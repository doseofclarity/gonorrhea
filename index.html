Sexually Transmitted Infection

Gonorrhea
("The Clap")

"Increasing antimicrobial resistance – treat with dual therapy."

Epidemiology & Overview

Gonorrhea – second most common bacterial STI caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae (Gram‑negative diplococcus).

Public Health Significance

  • High incidence globally; increasing antimicrobial resistance (multidrug‑resistant gonorrhea).
  • In the Philippines: reported cases from social hygiene clinics; underreporting due to asymptomatic infections and limited diagnostics.
  • Peak incidence in sexually active adolescents and young adults (15-30 years).
  • Major cause of PID, infertility, epididymitis, and neonatal ophthalmia.

Agent & Characteristics

Etiologic agent: Neisseria gonorrhoeae – Gram‑negative, kidney‑bean shaped diplococcus, fastidious, requires enriched media (chocolate agar).

Virulence factors: Pili, outer membrane proteins, lipooligosaccharide (LOS), IgA protease.

Increasing resistance: To penicillins, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and emerging resistance to cephalosporins.

Clinical Features & Diagnosis

Signs & Symptoms

  • Men (symptomatic ~90%): Urethritis – purulent urethral discharge (thick, yellow‑green), dysuria, meatal erythema.
  • Women (often asymptomatic ~50-80%): Cervicitis – increased vaginal discharge, dysuria, intermenstrual bleeding, postcoital bleeding; may be silent.
  • Rectal infection: May cause proctitis (pruritus, discharge, bleeding) or asymptomatic.
  • Pharyngeal infection: Usually asymptomatic; may cause sore throat.
  • Neonatal: Ophthalmia neonatorum (purulent conjunctivitis) within 2‑5 days after birth.
  • Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI): Fever, arthritis, tenosynovitis, skin pustules (rare).

Diagnosis

  • Gram stain: Intracellular Gram‑negative diplococci in urethral or endocervical smear (high sensitivity in symptomatic men).
  • Culture: Gold standard; allows antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Requires Thayer‑Martin or chocolate agar.
  • NAAT (Nucleic Acid Amplification Test): Highly sensitive; can detect from urine, vaginal, endocervical, rectal, pharyngeal swabs.
  • Recommended: NAAT for screening; culture for confirmed cases and resistance testing.
  • Dual testing for chlamydia is recommended (co‑infection common).

Transmission & Risk Factors

Mode of Transmission

  • Sexual contact (vaginal, anal, oral) with an infected partner.
  • Perinatal transmission – from infected mother to newborn during vaginal delivery (ophthalmia neonatorum).
  • Autoinoculation (hand to eye) possible but rare.
  • No transmission via fomites, toilet seats, or swimming pools.

Risk Factors

  • Age <25 years (especially adolescents)
  • Multiple sexual partners
  • Inconsistent condom use
  • Previous or coexisting STI (especially chlamydia)
  • Low socioeconomic status, lack of access to care
  • Men who have sex with men (MSM) – higher risk for pharyngeal and rectal gonorrhea

Prevention & Screening

Primary Prevention

  • Consistent and correct use of condoms.
  • Mutual monogamy with uninfected partner.
  • Reduction of number of sexual partners.
  • Abstinence.
  • Health education on STI transmission and prevention.

Screening Recommendations

  • Sexually active women <25 years – annual gonorrhea screening.
  • Women ≥25 years with risk factors.
  • Pregnant women – screen at first prenatal visit; repeat if high risk.
  • MSM – screen annually (urethral, rectal, pharyngeal).
  • Patients with HIV, or those attending STI clinics.
  • Partner notification and treatment.

Treatment & Complications

Antibiotic Therapy (DOH & CDC 2025 Guidelines)

  • Dual therapy (still recommended despite emerging resistance): Ceftriaxone 500mg IM single dose + Azithromycin 1g orally single dose.
  • Alternative for cephalosporin allergy: Gentamicin 240mg IM + Azithromycin 2g orally (or cefixime if mild).
  • Partner management: Treat all sexual partners within 60 days.
  • Test of cure: Not routinely recommended for uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea unless symptoms persist. However, pharyngeal infections should have test of cure 7-14 days after treatment.
  • Resistance surveillance: Critical – doxycycline and fluoroquinolones no longer recommended.
  • Pregnant women: Ceftriaxone + azithromycin; avoid quinolones.
  • Neonatal ophthalmia: Ceftriaxone 25-50mg/kg IV/IM single dose.

Complications (if untreated)

  • Women: Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) → infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain.
  • Men: Epididymitis → infertility (rare), prostatitis.
  • Neonatal: Ophthalmia neonatorum (blindness if untreated).
  • Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI): Arthritis, tenosynovitis, dermatitis, endocarditis, meningitis.
  • Pharyngeal and rectal infections may persist and cause local complications.

Prognosis & Antimicrobial Resistance

  • Excellent cure rate with appropriate dual therapy (>95%).
  • Multidrug‑resistant (MDR) and extensively drug‑resistant (XDR) strains emerging globally.
  • Treatment failure (especially ceftriaxone) reported in some regions.
  • Culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing essential for treatment failures.

PHN Role & Nursing Care

  • Health education: Promote condom use, partner reduction, STI awareness.
  • Screening promotion: Encourage annual screening for at‑risk populations (young women, MSM).
  • Partner notification & management: Assist in notifying sexual partners (last 60 days) and ensure treatment.
  • Expedited partner therapy (EPT): Provide prescriptions or medications to patients for their partners without prior examination (where permitted).
  • Prenatal care: Screen pregnant women; treat to prevent neonatal ophthalmia.
  • Treatment support: Counsel on medication adherence, need for IM ceftriaxone, and abstinence during treatment (7 days after single‑dose).
  • Prevention of reinfection: Emphasize retesting 3 months after treatment (high reinfection rate).
  • Antimicrobial resistance awareness: Educate on importance of completing treatment and not sharing antibiotics.
  • Reporting: Gonorrhea is a notifiable disease; report confirmed cases to local epidemiology unit.
